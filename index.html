I study how digital platforms shape consumer search and belief formation through the information they present, and develop structural methods to quantify the consequences for welfare and competition among firms and to evaluate platform regulation. Job Market Paper

Decoding Rankings: Consumer Search, Price Competition, and Platform Regulation

Using Expedia data, this paper shows that consumers substantially underestimate how much commission revenue shapes platform rankings. The gap between actual and perceived monetization makes ranking neutrality and transparency complements. Transparency alone reduces consumer welfare because attractive offers remain costly to discover; neutrality improves recommendations, but uncorrected beliefs limit its welfare gain. Together, the policies align improved recommendations with consumer beliefs and raise on-platform bookings by 26% after hotel repricing. Transparency also reverses the platform's revenue comparison in favor of neutrality.

Abstract

Online platforms often use monetized rankings: product positions reflect both predicted consumer value and commission revenue. Monetization can steer costly search toward high-commission offers, motivating two policies: neutrality that removes commission from the ranking criterion, and transparency that discloses the ranking's monetization. These policies may substitute if transparency suffices to let consumers discount steering, but complement if improved recommendations under neutrality remains misread with uncorrected beliefs. The determinant is therefore the gap between actual and perceived monetization. Using Expedia data, we estimate an equilibrium model of consumer search and hotel price competition that separately identifies the two. Consumers substantially underestimate monetization. Transparency corrects reliance on the monetized ranking but attractive options remains costly to discover, reducing consumer welfare. Neutrality instead facilitates search but uncorrected beliefs imply excessive skepticism, limiting the welfare gain. The policies are therefore complements: together they align what recommendations deliver with what consumers believe and raise on-platform bookings by 26% even after hotel repricing. The same mechanism also reverses the platform's revenue comparison: neutrality earns less commission with uncorrected beliefs but more under transparency.

Working Papers

Estimating Choice Models with Unobserved Expectations over Attributes

with Mathias Reynaert and Wenxuan Xu

Abstract

Agents often make choices based on expectations about uncertain attributes, yet the information used to form these expectations is typically unobserved by researchers. We develop two methods to estimate discrete choice models in which agents form expectations using heterogeneous and unobserved information sets. Preferences are point-identified by approximating the latent information structure with a finite mixture of information types, or set-identified with partial information about expectations. Both approaches apply to individual- and market-level data with minimal assumptions on expectation formation. Applications to firms' exporting decisions and consumers' automobile purchases show that accounting for unobserved information substantially affects estimated preferences.

Patent Challenge and Generic Entry

with Xin Zhang

Abstract

Pharmaceutical innovation depends on strong primary patents that allow originators to recoup R&D costs. However, drug companies often engage in evergreening that prolongs patent protection by filing follow-on patents with little therapeutic gain. We study a policy lever that works with market forces to screen out weak follow-on patents: the Hatch-Waxman Act, which incentivizes challenges to evergreening patents by granting the first successful challenger a period of marketing exclusivity. We investigate how the length of first-filer exclusivity shapes generic firms' incentives to initiate challenges, which can curb the extra monopoly protection created by evergreening while preserving incentives for genuine discovery and protecting consumer welfare through earlier generic entry. Using a two-stage structural model that endogenizes challenge and entry decisions, we estimate the fixed costs of generic entry with moment inequalities. We find that the current 180-day exclusivity raises challenge rates by about 4 percentage points. Extending exclusivity primarily activates challenges in markets that would otherwise go unchallenged: a two-year exclusivity increases the challenge rate to 15.38%. Effective exclusivity is highly heterogeneous across therapeutic classes: reaching a 20% challenge rate requires roughly two years for antimicrobials but less than one year for genitourinary drugs.

Specification Test for Models of Consumer Search

Abstract

Measuring consumer preferences requires knowing how consumers search. This paper develops a search-conduct test that distinguishes sequential from simultaneous search with differentiated products and outside options. The framework allows individual- and product-specific search costs and observed or unobserved searched attributes. Estimating both models with a unified maximum-likelihood approach, I develop a corrected Vuong test to compare their fit. Monte Carlo evidence shows that misspecified search conduct generates inconsistent preference estimates and that the test reliably selects the correct model.
